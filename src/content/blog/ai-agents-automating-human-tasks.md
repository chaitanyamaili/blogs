I spent part of last weekend using an AI agent to help me rewrite my LinkedIn profile, update my personal website, and scaffold a blog — this blog, actually. Work that would have taken me a full day of context-switching spread across a week took a few focused hours.

At one point I caught myself watching the agent edit files in my repo in real time, write copy I'd normally agonise over, and catch a broken Google Analytics script I'd forgotten about for months. And I had this weird, slightly uncomfortable thought: *this is a lot of what I used to call "my work".*

I don't say that to be dramatic. I'm still here, still making decisions, still the one who knows what actually needs to happen and why. But something has clearly shifted. And I think we — engineers especially — are doing a bad job of being honest about what.

## The execution gap is closing fast

For most of my career, being good at your job meant being able to *do* things well. Write clean code. Debug fast. Know the tools. Ship reliably.

Those skills still matter. But their value is changing — not disappearing, shifting. Because the gap between "has an idea" and "has a working implementation" is narrowing rapidly. An engineer with strong judgment and mediocre execution speed now produces roughly the same output as an engineer with mediocre judgment and strong execution speed, as long as the first one knows how to use the tools available.

That's a significant redistribution of leverage. And it has real consequences for how careers work, how teams are structured, and honestly, for how people feel about themselves professionally.

## What gets harder to ignore

Here's the thing about automation: it doesn't just change what you do. It makes certain gaps more visible.

If the agent can write the code, the scaffolding, the documentation — what's left? The decisions about *what* to build and *why*. The judgment about whether the output is actually right. The ability to spot the cases where the agent is confidently wrong. The context that can't be prompted — the institutional memory, the understanding of what happened three incidents ago and why a particular architectural choice would be a bad idea right now.

Those things have always mattered. What's changed is that they now matter *more*, in relative terms, because the other stuff got cheaper.

I've been a software engineer for 15 years. I've watched the things that made someone "good" shift multiple times — from knowing languages, to knowing frameworks, to knowing systems, to knowing architecture. This feels like another version of that same shift, just faster and more jarring than the previous ones.

## The identity part is real and we should talk about it

I want to be careful here because I think this gets dismissed too quickly in tech circles, where the reaction to any concern about automation is often "just upskill, adapt, you'll be fine."

Work is not just how people pay their bills. It's how they structure their days, build their sense of competence, justify their expertise, connect with other people. A meaningful chunk of professional identity is tied up in *being the person who can do the thing*. When the thing gets automated, that identity doesn't automatically transfer to something else.

I'm not talking about existential crisis — I'm talking about the more ordinary, daily version of this. The engineer who's proud of their ability to write fast, clean code, noticing that an agent can now produce equivalent output in seconds. The writer who's spent years developing a voice, watching AI generate passable drafts. The analyst who's built a career on being the person who can make sense of messy data, seeing that get commoditised.

The economic argument — "new jobs will be created, humans will move up the value chain" — is probably true in the long run. But the long run is made up of a lot of short runs, and the short run for a lot of people is disorienting in ways that "just adapt" doesn't fully acknowledge.

## What I think actually changes, specifically for engineers

Let me be concrete about what I observe changing, day to day, in my own work:

**The debugging loop gets faster, and that changes how I think.** I'm willing to try more approaches because the cost of each attempt is lower. That sounds like a small thing but it actually changes my problem-solving process — I'm more experimental, less committed upfront to the "right" approach.

**Writing is no longer the bottleneck it was.** Documentation, design docs, post-mortems — I still write them, but the activation energy required is much lower. Which means I do it more. Which is probably net positive for my team.

**The bar for "good enough to ship" has changed.** An agent can produce a working first pass faster than I can spec something carefully. That creates a new kind of risk: moving fast through the wrong problem. The judgment about whether you're building the right thing matters more, not less, when the building part gets faster.

**Cross-functional communication is getting compressed.** When I can turn a rough idea into a working prototype in an afternoon, the feedback loop between "thought" and "thing other people can react to" shrinks. That's mostly good. It does mean the bottleneck in getting things done shifts toward getting alignment, not toward building.

## What doesn't change — and this is the important part

Here's what I keep coming back to: the things that are hardest to automate are the things that were always the most important, just not always the most *visible*.

Trust. Someone has to own the outcome, and ownership requires being accountable to other humans, not just to a system. When something goes wrong — and things go wrong — the question "whose judgment was this?" still lands on a person.

Direction and taste. Agents are extraordinary at executing well-defined tasks. They're much weaker at knowing which task is worth doing, or at making judgment calls when the constraints are genuinely ambiguous. The value of people who are good at this — who can look at a situation with real context and say "actually no, we should do this instead" — goes up, not down.

Culture. The way a team operates, the norms it maintains, the kind of work it values — none of that emerges from an agent. It comes from people, from decisions made over time, from the cumulative effect of how leadership behaves and what gets rewarded.

The deeper point: the skills that are most resistant to automation are the skills that have always been the mark of genuinely senior, genuinely mature engineers. Not just the technical ones — the judgment, the communication, the knowing-when-to-push-back. The automation wave is, in some ways, just making more obvious what was always true: that those things are what actually differentiate people at the highest levels.

## The question I'm sitting with

I don't have a tidy conclusion here. I think that's appropriate — this is a genuinely complex transition happening faster than our institutions and our sense of professional identity can comfortably absorb.

The question I keep returning to is this: **when the doing gets cheap, what do you want to be the person who decides?**

That's not a rhetorical question. It's a practical one. Because the answer shapes what you invest in, what you pay attention to, and honestly, how you think about your own career over the next decade.

For me, the answer is something like: the person who makes good decisions about what infrastructure should look like, who helps teams think through hard technical problems, who can look at a system and see what's missing. That's what I'm orienting toward — not because the hands-on stuff doesn't matter, but because I think the leverage has shifted.

Your answer might be different. But I'd encourage you to have one.
